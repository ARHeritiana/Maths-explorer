Equidecomposability of Polygons

TL;DR. Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwien theorem: two polygons have equal area if and only if one can be cut into finitely many polygonal pieces that, moved by rigid motions (no reflection), reassemble into the other. Below, any pair P, Q is transformed through a common rectangle R.

Method

Claim. P ~ Q (equidecomposable) ⟺ area(P) = area(Q). Necessity is immediate (rigid motions preserve area). Sufficiency follows from three lemmas and transitivity.

  1. Polygon → triangles. Ear-clipping triangulation (any simple polygon admits one).
  2. Triangle → rectangle (3 pieces). Take the longest side as base, cut at mid-height, split the top triangle by its altitude, rotate each half by 180° about the midpoints of the slanted sides. Result: base × h/2.
  3. Rectangle a×b → rectangle W×(ab/W). (i) Slide strips of width a to shear the rectangle into a parallelogram whose slanted side has length W. (ii) Re-read the same parallelogram with base W and slide strips of width W to get a rectangle.
  4. Stack all rectangles of width W into one rectangle R of size W×A/W, with A the common area.

Transitivity. P ~ R and Q ~ R imply P ~ Q: superpose the cut patterns of P→R and R→Q (common refinement); this yields finitely many pieces mapping P onto Q. The animation plays the chain P → R, then the chain Q → R in reverse.

Remarks. Both polygons are rescaled to area 500 so the hypothesis holds. The construction favours clarity over minimal piece count. The result fails in ℝ³ (Dehn, 1900: the cube and regular tetrahedron of equal volume are not scissors-congruent, solving Hilbert’s third problem). Squaring the disc needs non-polygonal pieces (Tarski’s circle-squaring problem; Laczkovich, 1990).
