explained.

Use real specifications of London’s observation wheel to explore circular motion, cosine graphs, inequalities and derivatives, with six fully worked solutions.

London Eye adaptation · Interactive exercise · Works offline
Read the adapted exercise Based on published London Eye dimensions

The London Eye is an observation wheel on the South Bank of the River Thames. Its published overall height is 135 metres, its wheel diameter is 120 metres, and one full revolution takes approximately 30 minutes.

Idealized mathematical assumptions: Model a marked point travelling around the rim of a perfectly circular wheel, with a highest point 135 m above a reference ground level. Assume the wheel turns uniformly and takes exactly 30 minutes per revolution. The point begins at the lowest position when t = 0. Ignore the size and external mounting of the real capsules.

The height h metres of this model point above the reference level after t minutes is h(t) = a cos(bt) + d, for t ≥ 0. Angles are in radians.

  1. Determine the values of a, b and d.
  2. Find the height after 5 minutes.
  3. Find the first time when the height is 120 metres. Give your answer to two decimal places.
  4. For how long in the first 30 minutes is the height at least 120 metres? Give your answer to two decimal places.
  5. Find h′(t).
  6. Hence find the maximum rate of increase of height, in metres per minute.

Provenance: Dimensions come from the official London Eye history and facts; the approximate 30-minute rotation comes from its official support page. The starting position, exact period and circle-height interpretation are exercise assumptions, not independently measured capsule motion.

02 · Understand the solutions

Six questions, step by step.

Select a part to focus the graph and read its worked solution.

Part (a) · Build the cosine model

Determine a, b and d.

Distinguish what is given about the real wheel from what follows in our idealized circle model.

Amplitude: half the diameter.

The circle's radius is 120 ÷ 2 = 60 metres. Thus |a| = 60.

|a| = 60
Midline: midway between the modeled top and bottom.

If the model circle reaches 135 m at the top, its bottom is 135 − 120 = 15 m. The centre is 15 + 60 = 75 m.

d = 75
Period: 30 minutes.

Cosine completes one cycle when its angle increases by 2π radians.

b = 2π/30 = π/15
Starting from the bottom determines the sign.

At t = 0, cos(0) = 1; h(0) = 15. Hence a + 75 = 15, so a = −60.

h(t) = 75 − 60 cos(πt/15)
a = −60,   b = π/15,   d = 75.
Visual check: h(0) = 15 m, h(15) = 135 m, h(30) = 15 m. These are values of the idealized model.
Part (b) · Substitute a time

How high after 5 minutes?

Replace t with 5 in the model; the angle becomes π/3 radians.

Substitute.
h(5) = 75 − 60 cos(5π/15)
= 75 − 60 cos(π/3)
Evaluate the cosine.

Because cos(π/3) = 1/2:

h(5) = 75 − 60 × 1/2 = 45
At 5 minutes, the modeled height is 45 m.
Notice: 5 minutes is one sixth of the 30-minute period; the point has risen 30 m from its model minimum.
Part (c) · Solve a trigonometric equation

When does it first reach 120 m?

Equate h(t) to the threshold, solve for cosine, and use inverse cosine in radians.

Write the equation.
75 − 60 cos(πt/15) = 120
cos(πt/15) = −45/60 = −3/4
Choose the first angle.

The first solution from angle 0 is θ = arccos(−3/4), approximately 2.418858 radians.

πt/15 = arccos(−3/4)
Convert the angle to minutes.
t₁ = (15/π) arccos(−3/4)
≈ 11.55 minutes
First reaches 120 m at about 11.55 min.
Symmetry: the second crossing is at t₂ = 30 − t₁ ≈ 18.45 minutes, on the descending side of the wheel.
Part (d) · Solve a trigonometric inequality

How long at or above 120 m?

The graph's orange area highlights the part of the revolution above the threshold.

Translate “at least” into an inequality.
75 − 60 cos(πt/15) ≥ 120
cos(πt/15) ≤ −3/4

Remember to reverse the inequality when dividing by −60.

Find the two crossings.

The first crossing is t₁ ≈ 11.5492 min, and the return crossing is t₂ = 30 − t₁ ≈ 18.4508 min.

11.5492… ≤ t ≤ 18.4508…
Subtract the endpoints.
Duration = t₂ − t₁
≈ 18.4508 − 11.5492 = 6.90 min
The modeled height is ≥ 120 m for ≈ 6.90 min.
Why no extra time at the endpoints? Including exactly the two crossing instants does not alter an interval's duration.
Part (e) · Apply the chain rule

Find the instantaneous vertical rate h′(t).

This is the slope of the height graph, measured in metres per minute.

Differentiate cos(bt).

The derivative is −b sin(bt). The negative coefficient in the height function cancels this negative sign.

h′(t) = −60[−(π/15) sin(πt/15)]
Simplify.
60 × π/15 = 4π
h′(t) = 4π sin(πt/15) m/min.
Compare the graph and wheel: positive means rising; negative means descending. At the modeled lowest and highest points, the vertical rate is momentarily zero.
Part (f) · Maximize the derivative

What is the maximum rate of increase?

Sine cannot exceed 1. The steepest positive slope appears when the point is midway up the ascending side.

Use the derivative.
h′(t) = 4π sin(πt/15)
Maximum possible sine value is 1.
max h′(t) = 4π × 1 = 4π
≈ 12.57 m/min
Find when it happens.

At πt/15 = π/2, so t = 7.5 minutes.

t = 7.5 min; h(t) = 75 m
Maximum upward rate = 4π ≈ 12.57 m/min.
Signed rate versus speed: at t = 7.5 min the vertical rate is +12.57 m/min; at t = 22.5 min it is −12.57 m/min. Both have the same magnitude, but only the former is the maximum signed rate. This is an idealized model result, not a measured operating speed.
✓
The key idea

Radius gives amplitude; centre height gives vertical shift; revolution time gives period; the starting position determines the sign. To answer the later parts, evaluate, solve, differentiate, and interpret the same model rather than memorizing separate rules.

03 · Real data versus model

Where the numbers come from

Reported data: 135 m overall height, 120 m wheel diameter, 32 passenger capsules and a roughly 30-minute revolution. These are factual specifications, not measurements taken by this lesson.

Modelling choices: approximate the vertical motion by a point on a 120 m circular path, use the published 135 m as the model’s maximum, choose a common reference level, treat the 30-minute rotation as exact, and start at its lowest point. Consequently the 15 m minimum and all calculated heights and speeds belong to the model; they should not be interpreted as measured positions or clearances of the real capsules. The real capsules are mounted outside the rim and remain upright.

  • The London Eye, History and Facts (official website): 135 m height, 120 m wheel diameter.
  • The London Eye, What is the London Eye? (official support): approximately 30-minute rotation.
  • The London Eye, FAQs (official website): 32 pods.
  • Steven Chilton Architects, The London Eye: capsules on external bearings and the wheel’s A-frame structure.
